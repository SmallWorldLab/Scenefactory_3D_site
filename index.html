Abstract

Scalable driving simulators typically execute vehicle commands using prescribed behavioral or kinematic rules, overlooking the physics of tire–road interfaces, thereby limiting their ability to capture how adverse road and environmental conditions alter vehicle execution and propagate through traffic.

To address this limitation, we present SceneFactory-3D, a GPU-batched, physics-grounded multi-agent driving simulator. Vehicles execute acceleration and steering commands via suspension- and friction-limited forces evaluated at each wheel-contact point. Spatially varying friction, per-world 3D heightfields, gravity, and rigid contact consistently govern wheel motion and chassis collisions. Per-world terrain isolation and GPU batching enable SceneFactory-3D to run matched physical counterfactuals in parallel: traffic scenario setup and vehicle controllers remain fixed while only the road condition changes, enabling the resulting closed-loop effects to be evaluated across parallel worlds.

To demonstrate the advantage of SceneFactory-3D-enabled counterfactual evaluation, we conduct an empirical study on vehicle controllers' sensitivity to road conditions. We study three learned-policy families on 1,024 matched 12-vehicle worlds per condition, and two classical planners on a shared 32-world subset, across 21 friction and grade conditions. When friction drops from 1.0 to 0.18, the share of vehicles that clear the work zone safely falls by 6 to 90 percentage points across learned policies (18–19 for classical planners), and near-collision situations become more frequent for every learned policy.

The Simulator

SceneFactory-3D extends SceneFactory, a GPU-vectorized Isaac Lab platform whose articulated vehicles drive on flat roads. It replaces the vehicle model with a custom tire-force model evaluated at every wheel–road contact, gives each parallel world its own terrain, and exposes one canonical route interface to classical and learned controllers alike. A matched physical counterfactual then rolls out the same scene, initial state, routes and controller population on a reference road and on a changed road; the difference in outcome is the total closed-loop effect of the change within the simulator.

01

Canonical routes

Each route is planned once from the road layout, without reference to any controller, and checked for continuity, drivable bounds, closure clearance and a curvature budget. Merges blend two lane offsets with a quintic that has zero curvature at both ends. The route is a shared spatial reference, not a shared trajectory: controllers still choose speed, gaps and timing.

road layout → route r(u) = c(u) + o(u) n(u)

02

Force-based vehicle & road

Each vehicle is a free rigid chassis on four raycast suspension legs with rotating wheels. A custom Warp kernel computes suspension, tire, drive, brake, steering and drag forces at every 240 Hz step. All controllers share one actuation bridge, so a requested deceleration or turn is realized only as far as the road's grip allows.

command a, δ → wheel forces → realized motion

03

Per-world terrain & matching

Wheel rays, route and preview queries, and chassis collision geometry all resolve the terrain variant assigned to their world, and collisions never cross worlds. Each run records scenario and route digests, the initial state, terrain hash and checkpoint identity; a pair is admitted only if it differs in the declared intervention alone.

same (scene, controllers) × different road

04

GPU batching

An Isaac Lab DirectRLEnv steps every world together with Newton's XPBD solver, CUDA graphs, 240 Hz physics and 60 Hz actuation, without rendering. Loop time per world stays nearly flat from 64 to 1,024 twelve-vehicle worlds on one GPU.

Controller-Sensitivity Benchmark

One held-out lane-closure task, five controller regimes and 21 physical conditions, evaluated on matched worlds.

Task

A straight two-lane road whose right lane closes at x = 150 m. Of 12 vehicles, five start in the closing lane and must merge between 50 and 110 m; seven follow the open lane. All 12 are driven by the evaluated controller with shared parameters, so a vehicle that slides or brakes late changes its neighbors' situation through the same dynamics. Episodes last 66 s.

Valid passage — the primary outcome — requires crossing a counting line at 170 m before any closure incursion, contact, off-road departure or fault. A controller's sensitivity is its change in valid passage relative to its own reference road on the same worlds; it is not a quality ranking.

Physical conditions

Every world shares a flat reference road up to 40 m, so all begin under identical physics.

  • Reference: flat road, μsurface = 1.0
  • 8 friction levels on the closing lane from 40 to 180 m, covering the merge: μ = 0.70, 0.50, 0.35, 0.25, 0.18, 0.12, 0.08, 0.05
  • 8 grades over the full road width, +4% to −8%, with smooth transitions
  • 4 compositions of grades −4.5% and +3% with μ = 0.35 and 0.18

At μ ≤ 0.08 a vehicle braking fully on patch entry at 16 m/s cannot stop within the patch; these two levels are treated as an extreme-severity group.

Reduced friction lowered valid passage for every checkpoint: by 0.06–0.90 at μ = 0.18 (every interval excluding zero) and 0.00–0.70 at μ = 0.35. The vehicle equations fix how much braking a patch removes, not how much a controller population loses — and that loss depended more on the training seed than on the training regime.

On the reference road nine of twelve learned checkpoints pass 0.96–1.00 of vehicles. The three seed-3 checkpoints (one parent) pass 0.28–0.53 because in nearly every world a vehicle weaving at 27–33 m/s rolls over and the world reset censors the rest. Classical planners ran in 32-world batches, so classical-vs-learned differences are read only when large.

Finding 1 · friction

Friction Losses Spread to the Unchanged Lane

The low-friction patch covers only the closing lane, yet open-lane passage fell from 0.82–0.88 to 0.28–0.62 at μ = 0.18. Open-lane collisions rose only from about 1% to 3–6%: most of the loss is censoring, when a sliding closing-lane vehicle overturns and resets its world. Within a family, seed ranges at μ = 0.18 (0.41–0.76) exceed the differences between family means.

Finding 2 · interaction safety

Risk Rises before Failures Occur

Within the valid-driving window, the share of vehicles reaching TTC below 1.5 s rose for all twelve learned checkpoints at every μ ≤ 0.12 (36 of 36 intervals exclude zero), even though valid driving per vehicle fell from about 53 s to 16–25 s. Normalized by time, exposure below 1.5 s rose for all twelve already at μ = 0.18.

In the 60 Hz subset, braking need above the capacity left after lateral tire use 1–2 s earlier raised the hazard of a vehicle's own failure 11- to 30-fold at μ ≤ 0.12, and 10- to 25-fold while TTC was still above 1.5 s. These are associations, not causal effects.

Finding 3 · grade

The Tested Grades Rarely Changed the Task

Grade changed passage by at most 0.011 for seven of twelve checkpoints. The largest loss, 0.15 at −8% for L1 seed 0, came with collisions rising from 4.9% to 22.1%. Grade–friction compositions did not depart resolvably from additivity.

Finding 4 · training exposure and preview

Preview Did Not Produce Anticipation of Low Friction

L1 and L0-T differ only in the preview. L1 lost less passage at μ = 0.35–0.18 in three of four seeds, but mostly through seed 0 (+0.72 at 0.18, against +0.13 for seed 1 and near zero for seed 2 at ceiling), with mixed signs at μ ≤ 0.12; across seeds the contrast is unresolved. Speed 20 m before the patch changed by only −0.2 to +1.3 m/s for every checkpoint. L1 did anticipate grade, entering a −8% slope 1.0–2.4 m/s slower.

Classical planners fall inside the learned range. PDM-Closed-17 passed every vehicle on the reference road, lost 0.19 at μ = 0.18, and still passed 0.52–0.54 at μ ≤ 0.08 — at least 0.13 more than any other regime — but at a far higher per-world planning cost.

Limitations

The vehicle equations fix how much braking a patch removes, not how much a controller population loses. That loss depended more on training seed than on regime, so one policy per method could have produced any ordering. The simulator is verified but not calibrated, and simulator safety measures are not evidence about real-road safety.

Current scope

  • One closure geometry and one policy architecture.
  • Noise-free preview for L1.
  • Four seeds cannot resolve family differences of the observed size.
  • Frozen L0 vs. L0-T includes additional training.
  • Whole-world resets couple vehicle outcomes, and batch composition perturbs individual trajectories.
